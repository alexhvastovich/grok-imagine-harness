# Film-informed action sequences

Use for action filmmaking and storyboards: gunfights, cover, chases, close combat,
grenade interruptions and powered movement. Requested by Alex on 2026-10-27 after
a Marine fight draft became two stationary figures exchanging fire.

These are adaptable filmmaking patterns, not exact reconstructions of the films.
Sources are cinematographer/director/stunt-team interviews read on 2026-10-27;
no frame-by-frame viewing or precise film timecodes are claimed. The coverage
recipes and science-fiction adaptations below are our synthesis. Use original
imagery, blocking and sound; do not import the films' footage, actors or scores.

## Select a sequence

| Pattern | Film reference | Useful for | Dramatic change |

| A1 | The Matrix — lobby | Columns, lateral runs, destructible cover | Protection is progressively stripped away |
| A2 | Heat — street battle | Purposeful relocation, pressured escape | Each move gains ground or loses an escape route |
| A3 | John Wick — home invasion | Compact, readable contact choreography | A weapon advantage changes through physical contact |
| A4 | John Wick: Chapter 4 — Osaka / stairs | Different fighting styles, changing levels | Terrain creates a disadvantage the fighter must solve |

Choose one principal pattern and, when helpful, a complementary reversal. Do not
force every film into a short. Keep user-requested outcomes, tone and capabilities.

For requested helmet-camera, first-person or grounded combat framing, also read
[POV camera study](pov-camera-study.md). It includes inspected Ukraine footage
references and corrects the failure of isolated pillars staged in a frontal row.

## A1 — Column-to-column firefight

**Source observation:** Bill Pope describes the lobby's stone columns breaking
apart under fire and action taking place in side colonnades. The directors discuss
storyboarding individual beats and giving fights their own narrative arcs.
[ASC interview](https://theasc.com/article/flashback-the-matrix-cinematography/).

**Adaptable coverage:**

1. Wide: establish both fighters, two usable columns and the gap between them.
2. Medium behind cover: fighter leans out, fires, withdraws when return impacts
   reach the exposed edge. The column visibly blocks the opponent's line of sight.
3. Reverse medium: the opponent changes position rather than shooting the same
   empty place indefinitely.
4. Lateral wide: a sprint/dive crosses the exposed gap; impacts follow the route.
5. Detail or medium: the next cover chips, then loses a substantial section.
6. Wide payoff: the lost protection forces a new choice, flank or retreat.

Keep a map of named columns and which faces protect each actor. Preserve damage
and rubble. Heavy armor means a weighted dash and hard landing, not an acrobatic
cartwheel by default. Dust may bridge a cut; it must not hide an unexplained move.

Columns must belong to believable architecture and block the opponent's view,
not merely conceal the actor from the camera. Avoid a symmetrical row of isolated
stumps in an empty yard. Use connected beams, walls, doorways and offset depths
as the location calls for them; establish an actual firing sightline.

## A2 — Movement with an objective

**Source observation:** Michael Mann describes detailed rehearsal for Heat and
ties the street battle to the robbers' need to get out and the police's opposing
objective. The characters' skills and motivations determine the choreography.
[DGA interview](https://www.dga.org/craft/dgaq/issues/1201-winter-2012/dga-interview-michael-mann).

**Adaptable coverage:** show the destination in a wide; show the obstacle from
the actor's position; cut to a brief firing beat that changes the pressure; hold
a wide or lateral track through the actual relocation; reveal its consequence.
An ammunition interruption can force a retreat or expose a flank. With multiple
characters, alternate their roles visibly; with a duel, establish why the opponent
cannot immediately stop the move. Do not invent allies just to use the pattern.

Use gun reports, ricochets, breath and a brief drop in firing to define rhythm.
Different weapons should have distinguishable visual and audio weight. Avoid a
continuous identical burst over every shot. This is narrative design, not a claim
that the fictional tactics are real-world instruction or mechanically optimal.

## A3 — Readable weapon/contact reversal

**Source observation:** Darrin Prescott discusses the first John Wick's physical
choreography and operating the camera as part of the coordinated performance.
[Film Independent interview](https://www.filmindependent.org/blog/bonus-stunt-coordinator-darrin-prescott-dissects-john-wicks-best-action-scenes/).

**Adaptable coverage:** medium two-shot with both hands and the weapon visible;
one clear contact redirects the weapon; cut on that contact only if the next angle
matches hands and balance; return to a medium/wide for the resulting fall or loss
of position. Use inserts for a decisive prop change, not to disguise every impact.

For armored characters, a weapon break, shoulder collision or knockback can serve
the same dramatic role as a human disarm. Establish proximity first. Keep the
fighter's mass and the opponent's resistance visible. Avoid limbs fusing into
weapons or a reversal that occurs only between unrelated endpoints.

## A4 — Terrain and contrasting abilities

**Source observation:** Jeremy Marinas describes distinct movement styles for
Osaka's characters, varied levels and an objective/disadvantage driving the stairs.
[Fight-coordinator interview](https://www.motionpictures.org/2023/03/john-wick-chapter-4-fight-coordinator-jeremy-marinas-on-building-balletic-mayhem-with-keanu-reeves-co/).

**Adaptable coverage:** establish the obstacle and destination together; contrast
one fighter's fast improvisation with another's economical heavy movement; give
the first a brief advantage; let the second use a previously established ability
to change level or angle; re-establish both positions before the payoff.

For a jump-pack fighter this can become ground cover -> visible ignition -> wide
powered arc -> weighted landing on a flank. That is an original adaptation, not
a claim about John Wick's equipment. Keep the pack visible on the master and in
earlier shots; no surprise equipment appearing only at takeoff.

## A5 — Pursuit, obstruction, escalation

**Source observation:** Adam Greenberg discusses Terminator 2's chase emphasis,
multiple camera positions, rear-projection work and the long freeway helicopter
pursuit. This source supports broad pursuit design, not a verified shot list of
the canal sequence.
[ASC interview](https://theasc.com/article/terminator-2-he-said-he-039-d-be-back/).

**Adaptable coverage:** wide shows subject, pursuer and escape direction; tracking
shot establishes relative speed; obstacle insert introduces the next problem;
wide shows the actual avoidance/impact; reverse reveals whether the pursuer is
delayed, destroyed or still coming. Escalate through a changed situation, not
merely a larger explosion. Keep travel direction, speed, distances and damage.

For a short duel, an armored advance or airborne pursuit can replace vehicles.
Preserve the character's established limitations and the user's intended winner.

## Original connective sequences

Use these to join the researched patterns when the story calls for them. They
are not attributed to a specific film or promised to reproduce a real stunt.

**Grenade between bursts:** firing stops for a reason -> visible grenade in hand
-> throw/release in a medium shot -> separate view shows its travel or landing
near established cover -> opponent reacts and gets behind protection -> blast
changes that cover -> wide reveals the new positions. Keep grenade ownership,
throwing hand, one grenade instance and persistent damage. Do not let it explode
before release or let characters ignore the established threat. Omit exact real
device mechanics and timers unless the story genuinely needs them.

**Real-time gunfire:** the creator's Marine correction excludes slow visible bullets
crossing the aisle. Use compact muzzle flashes and immediate localized impacts;
keep airborne rounds, rocket bodies, long tracer streaks and suspended glowing
bolts out of the input images as well as the motion prompt. Inspect individual
rounds at full resolution: the T02 contact sheets hid a conspicuous projectile
that full-size frame47 exposed. A timestamp pin controls an action state, not the
physical speed of every effect, and does not by itself cure bullet-time motion.

**Jump-pack flank:** establish pack and obstacle -> crouch/ignition and downwash
-> powered departure -> wide tracks one readable arc -> landing with momentum
and dust -> opponent turns toward the new angle -> consequence. Use separate
shots for takeoff, travel and landing when one short generation cannot carry
them cleanly. Jump changes geometry; it is not decorative hovering while firing.

**False victory / distant strike:** defeated opponent stays down -> brief quiet
hold -> cut to distant observer -> readable targeting cue -> victor notices too
late -> establish approaching threat -> impact -> specific evidence of outcome.
The observer remains in the established safe location. No new escape ability
should undo the locked ending without the user's direction.

## Translate into the Grok packet

For each selected pattern record: shot IDs; objective; initial positions; visible
obstacle; action/reaction; changed advantage; camera size/lens/height/movement;
screen direction; FIRST/LAST states; prop/hand and damage continuity; sound;
intended duration and next cut. Sketch the set before generating frames.

A recipe is a sequence of shots, not one overloaded generation prompt. Use one
camera setup and one principal physical change per job. Design all inspected
pairs first under the normal harness gate. For continuous action reuse actual
accepted ending frames; new angles need matching independent pairs.

Before motion, check that the viewer can answer: where is each fighter, what
protects them, why do they move, and what changed? Reject frame pairs with a gun
aimed away from the opponent, meaningless cover, hidden release/contact, equipment
appearing from nowhere, reset damage, or identical static poses across the fight.

## Action rhythm and source duration

The Marine first pass used six-second source takes. Alex subsequently allowed
shorter takes, or longer sources trimmed to the action. Choose a supported source
duration for the beat and inspect the actual motion; six seconds is not a standing
requirement. The final runtime remains open, with no preset sixty-second slot.

Carry action through the cuts: enter a take with intent/movement already present,
finish with a usable action handle, and avoid settling every six-second scene
into a posed stop. Cut on a turn, recoil, start of a run, crossing foreground cover,
throw, upward look or landing when the next angle continues the same event.
Match action phase, direction, body/hand/prop positions and impact timing.

Select edit lengths from the actual accepted motion. Mix brief reactions and
inserts with shots long enough to read a dash, grenade consequence, flight path
or major explosion. More cuts and more shake alone do not make action dynamic.
Do not speed up a confusing stunt to disguise it; fix that take or its coverage.
Track in/out points, avoid duplicated action at joins, bridge gunfire/ambience
where useful, and reserve a quiet beat when the story needs the false victory.
Native six-second clip endpoints are not automatically the right edit points.

Specify real-time action timing when a model stretches the movement across the
whole source. Start with intent already present, complete a throw or short burst
early, and leave natural recovery/aftermath as edit handles. A LAST image with a
grenade still floating beside the hand can encourage a suspended prop; use a
physically later endpoint if that defect needs regeneration. Timing language is
a request, not proof that the model obeyed it. Inspect the rendered release,
flight, impact, cover and weapon line before accepting the replacement.
