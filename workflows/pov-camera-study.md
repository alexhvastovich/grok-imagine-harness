Requested by the user on 2026-10-27 to improve the Marine fight's angles and grounding.
Use this for a requested first-person/helmet-camera treatment, alongside the
[film action recipes](action-sequences.md). It is camera and production design,
not combat training or an analysis of battlefield effectiveness.

## Observations from the sampled Euronews frames

- Opening seconds: earth banks occupy much of the frame, leaving a narrow view
  of sky and branches; the camera is close to other people and protective terrain.
- Around six to nine seconds: nearby helmet/shoulder, ground and dust interrupt
  the view. The framing does not keep an opponent neatly centered.
- Around fourteen to twenty-five seconds: the rifle enters the lower/right
  foreground and changes angle as the camera orientation changes. Background
  branches and the ground remain important visual anchors.
- Around thirty-six to forty-seven seconds: the viewpoint changes between nearby
  people, gear and narrow terrain passages. Foreground occlusion conveys depth.
- Around fifty to fifty-nine seconds: a nearby helmet and high earth banks dominate
  the view while debris/dust briefly interrupts the sky.

These are sampled visual observations, not a reconstruction of the engagement or
claims about who is shooting whom. Do not infer unseen targets or successful hits.

## Translate into readable animation

Use short POV inserts within an established scene, not continuous uncontrolled
shake. An occasional wide or over-the-shoulder view should explain positions the
subjective image cannot. Only use a roll/jerk when the character's movement or an
impact motivates it; avoid constant equal-amplitude vibration.

Useful coverage:

1. Three-quarter establishing view down a real aisle or through a damaged doorway.
   Put cover at different depths; establish both sides before going subjective.
2. Over-the-shoulder view close behind armor, looking past the near edge of a
   wall/support. Let a portion of the shoulder or weapon occlude the foreground.
3. Brief first-person exposure: a narrow glimpse beyond cover, rifle partly at the
   lower edge, imperfect target visibility. Keep the principal story event legible.
4. Retreat/dip: the camera lowers so the wall, floor or shoulder blocks the view;
   dust/impact sound carries the interrupted action. Do not magically relocate.
5. A medium/wide reaction shows what changed: damaged protection, a new position,
   a grenade's consequence or a jump-pack fighter overhead.

For the grenade beat, show ownership and release in a readable external angle,
then cut to a short subjective reaction and the changed set. For the powered
flank, use an upward glance followed by a wide with a clear launch/landing path.
These are original fictional coverage choices, not instructions derived from real
troops' movements.

## Cover and angle checks

- Draw the line between opponent and actor. The cover must intersect it. Hiding
  a body behind a prop only from the viewer's angle does not demonstrate protection.
- Supports belong to a structure: surviving beam, roof bay, collapsed wall or
  loading dock. Avoid decorative isolated pillar stumps arranged like a stage.
- Use offset foreground/middle/background planes. Change view angle or shot size
  for a reason; a row of frontal full-body tableaux reads as posed character art.
- Establish light and sightlines before sparks/tracers. A gun must point into
  clear space and toward the relevant action, not into the actor's own cover.
- Track which side of a wall each body occupies. Occlusion must follow camera
  position; no weapons/body parts penetrating masonry.
- Choose readable fictional action over recreating identifiable people, injury
  scenes or footage from the references. Store research imagery in local QA only;
  final visuals are newly generated for the story.
